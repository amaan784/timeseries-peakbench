# Time Series Peakbench

Benchmarking load forecasters for battery peak shaving.

**[Live report](https://amaan784.github.io/timeseries-peakbench/)** · **[Full results](docs/results.md)** · **[Method](docs/method.md)**

When a commercial battery uses a load forecast to cut demand charges, how much money does forecast error actually cost? This repo answers in dollars rather than MAPE. It simulates a year of battery dispatch at 15-minute resolution under day-ahead forecasters, from a seasonal-naive baseline to Google's TimesFM and Mistral's Timestral, and three dispatch policies, on one synthetic office building and six real metered buildings.

The claim under test: most of the value is in a dispatch policy that degrades gracefully when the forecast is wrong, not in a better forecast. The experiment was built so that claim could fail, and in part it did.

Share of perfect-foresight savings each policy captured, across the six forecasters (synthetic building, one year at 15-minute resolution).

- **Policy beats forecaster.** With the forecaster fixed (Ridge), the three policies are $20k a year apart; with the policy fixed, the forecasters are $2.8k apart (6.9x).
- **The obvious policy is the worst one.** Threshold captures 16-24% of perfect-foresight savings; threshold + ratchet captures 62-67%.
- **Timing error breaks schedules.** A perfect forecast 30 minutes late costs the window policy $38k a year (72% of its savings); the meter-driven policies don't move.
- **No policy wins everywhere.** Without the equipment spikes, window comes out on top (67-79%) and threshold + ratchet drops to 43-54%.
- **Real meters.** On six metered buildings (UCI, Portugal, 2014), threshold + ratchet was the best policy on six of six, the policy spread beat the forecaster spread on six of six, and a perfect forecast 30 minutes off cost the window schedule 35-70% of its savings.
- **Better forecasts, little money.** The foundation models (TimesFM 3.0, TimesFM 2.5 and Timestral 209M) moved annual savings by at most $3.0k against ridge. The most accurate forecaster by MAPE, Timestral 209M (4.0% vs 5.7% for ridge), changed threshold + ratchet savings by +$1.3k a year.

## How it works

- **Buildings:** a synthetic Boston-area office (weekday occupancy, a summer cooling peak, short equipment spikes), plus six real 15-minute meters from UCI's ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014.
- **Tariff:** an illustrative Massachusetts-style time-of-use rate with a monthly demand charge on the single highest 15-minute interval.
- **Battery:** a 2-hour C&I battery with round-trip losses, state-of-charge limits and off-peak charging.
- **Forecasters:** seasonal naive, 28-day profile and ridge (refit daily), TimesFM 3.0, TimesFM 2.5 and Timestral 209M (zero-shot on CPU), and a perfect-foresight oracle as the ceiling. Each sees only data up to the midnight before the day it forecasts. TimeGPT is wired in too but needs an API key.
- **Policies:** *window* discharges on the forecast's schedule; *threshold* discharges whenever the meter crosses the day's target; *threshold + ratchet* also never spends energy shaving below a peak already set this month.

The details, the foundation-model setup and what the tests check are in [docs/method.md](docs/method.md).

## Quick start

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m src.evaluate        # synthetic building -> out/ (about 15 s)
python -m src.real_meters     # six real meters -> out/real/ (about 30 s)
python -m src.report          # HTML report and the generated results
streamlit run app.py          # interactive dashboard
```

No GPU or torch needed: the foundation-model forecasts are cached in `out/cache/`, and the latest results are committed, so the dashboard runs straight after the install. With `make`: `make all`, then `make app`.

## Limitations

The main building is synthetic; the six real meters are a check, not a proof. The tariff is illustrative, batteries don't degrade, dispatch is day-ahead only, and there are no confidence intervals, so forecaster differences of a few points are within noise. The full list is in [docs/method.md](docs/method.md#limitations).
