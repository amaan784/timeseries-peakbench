Choosing the dispatch policy is worth $20k a year. Choosing the forecaster is worth $2.8k.

When a commercial battery uses a day-ahead load forecast to cut demand charges, how much money does forecast error cost? A 500 kWh / 250 kW battery, an office building that peaks at 797 kW, a $22.00/kW monthly demand charge, five day-ahead forecasters and three dispatch policies, simulated at 15-minute resolution over 365 days.

Synthetic dataThe building load is generated (weekday occupancy, Boston-like weather, a summer cooling peak, random equipment spikes), not read from a meter. The tariff is illustrative, not a filed rate.
In short
  1. Policy beats forecaster. Holding the forecaster fixed (Ridge), the three dispatch policies are $20k a year apart. Holding the policy fixed (threshold + ratchet), the five forecasters are $2.8k apart. In every case the spread across policies exceeds the spread across forecasters, by 2.3x to 6.5x.
  2. The obvious threshold policy is the worst one. Setting each day's target as low as the forecast says the battery can hold captures only 16-22% of perfect-foresight savings. It spends the whole battery on the forecast's peak, so when the real peak is higher the battery is empty before it arrives: it ran out with the meter still above target on 47-72% of business days (the ratchet: 5-7%).
  3. Reading the meter only pays if the policy respects the bill. Adding one rule (never shave below a peak already set this month) lifts the same closed-loop policy to 62-67%, against 32-35% for a forecast-scheduled window.
  4. Timing error is what kills schedules. A perfect forecast running 30 minutes late costs the window policy $38k a year; the meter-driven policies don't move.
  5. But no policy wins everywhere. Remove the short equipment spikes from the same building and the ranking flips: the window schedule captures 67-77%, the ratchet 43-54%. Spikes are what a forecast can't place and a meter can catch; on a smooth load, the risk is under-forecasting the level, and a schedule bounded by its plan degrades more gently.
  6. Foundation models did not change the answer. On the synthetic building, neither foundation model beat ridge on day-ahead peak error (71 and 66 vs 65 kW mean absolute error), despite a better average MAPE. Under threshold + ratchet they moved annual savings by +$893 (TimesFM 3.0), +$2.8k (TimesFM 2.5), against $20k between policies. 08
Method and limits

How the numbers were made

Each forecaster issues a 96-interval forecast for day D using only data up to midnight before D; a test poisons the future and checks that no forecast changes. Foundation models see a 28-day trailing context, zero-shot, and their forecasts are cached per (model, data fingerprint, date). The battery has 88% round-trip efficiency (square root on each leg) and a state of charge kept between 10% to 95%. It charges only off-peak, never above the day's target, and paced to refill before the next on-peak window (8:00 to 21:00 on business days, $0.19/kWh; $0.13/kWh otherwise). Savings are full-bill: demand plus time-of-use energy.

Limitations

  • The main study is one generated building. The six real meters are a check, not a census: anonymous Portuguese clients, one year each, with the illustrative MA tariff applied to them.
  • Illustrative tariff. No customer charges, ratchets, coincident-peak or capacity charges.
  • No battery degradation, so cycling looks free and the threshold policy's extra cycles are not penalised.
  • No grid-services or wholesale revenue stacked on top of bill savings.
  • A single meter and a single battery; no portfolio effects.
  • Day-ahead only: no intraday re-forecasting, which a production controller would do.
